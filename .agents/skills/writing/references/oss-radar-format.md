# OSS Radar format

Current series contract, November 14, 2026. The reference issues are [#1: Vercel's Winter 2026 cohort](../../../../packages/blog/posts/002-oss-radar-01-vercel-winter-2026-cohort.md) and [#14: November Show HN launches](../../../../packages/blog/posts/045-oss-radar-14-november-show-hn.md). Preserve their sources → projects → interpretation → distillation progression. Sources may be one bounded cohort or a curated mix of new discoveries and returning projects, with a clear selection rationale. A strong engineering explanation is not automatically an issue of this series.

## Reader promise

Assemble a coherent selection from declared sources. Help the reader discover worthwhile projects, understand what makes them distinct, and form a judgment about the set. Return to earlier participants when their history, recent changes or changed circumstances make the follow-up useful. Original code reading, trials, discussions and comparisons should sharpen those judgments. Neither README summaries nor a collection of unrelated technical tutorials delivers the promise.

## Recurring article structure

1. **Opening:** name the cohort and lead with concrete projects or the strongest supported finding. Make the useful result immediate. Keep planning conversations and a research diary outside the article.
2. **Sources and selection:** identify where projects came from, the author/date or time window, population/sample where defined, selection reasons and consequential biases. A curated mix is not a representative census. An HN appearance is not a project's creation date. Verify licenses before calling a selected project open source.
3. **The projects:** use `### Project: the job it does` entries. Give projects unequal space according to their importance and evidence, while answering the common questions below. Compact grouping is useful when several projects genuinely share a category.
4. **What this cohort shows:** explain patterns supported by the reviewed projects, including counterexamples, sample bias and a credible competing explanation. Do not make a market-wide trend from a selected launch sample.
5. **The distillation:** make a few consequential judgments: what is useful now, what merits a closer look, what adoption costs or hard questions remain. Explain the reasons. Follow-up work is optional and concrete; it must not repeat the project summaries.
6. **Sources:** concise primary-source/reproduction links and useful term explanations. Keep consequential citations beside their claims too.

These are stable editorial functions and the default section order. Keep headings plain. Do not reinstate stock prognosis labels, forced controversy, unsupported forecasts or an identical checklist printed under every project.

## Questions every project entry must answer

- **What problem and whose work?** State the task, intended user and practical importance in ordinary words.
- **What is technically distinct?** Trace a consequential called path beyond the README. Explain what it replaces, wraps, extends and relies on; a language/framework list is insufficient.
- **How did it get here?** For a returning project, compare the earlier article's dated position with checked historical/current source, releases and consequential work in recent weeks. Distinguish a pivot from routine changes. Do not treat an earlier article's unsourced statement as proof.
- **What supports the interpretation?** Use pinned source, tests, attributed maintainer/user discussion and meaningful trials where they change the judgment. Distinguish inspected, reproduced and reported evidence; retain failures and contrary results.
- **What is the take?** Explain why this project matters, who could try it, what ongoing responsibilities remain, and the hard question or condition that would change the advice. Directional possibilities need their actual state: stated, in progress, enabled or speculative.

Use those questions to research and review. In reader copy, prefer connected paragraphs like #14, with a small comparison or worked example when useful. A focused experiment belongs next to the conclusion it changes; longer protocols, fixtures and measurements stay in linked research records.

The [participant due-diligence workflow](oss-radar-due-diligence.md) owns discovery sources, historical/current comparisons, claim audits, experiments, sentiment and completion evidence. Plan substantive time with each featured project, often hours when the important question requires it; a readme scan or a passing setup does not finish a review. Record actual effort when known without using time as proof of quality.

## Weekly acceptance

Finish the cohort inventory and selection before finalizing its thesis. Read every selected project's relevant implementation, license and discussion; record unavailable testimony honestly. A representative trial may be narrow, but a setup failure is not a successful product evaluation. Do not promise every repository a full benchmark or require an artificial tool build for each entry.

Before release, check that the article still covers the selected source, all featured entries earn their space, and the distillation follows from the project evidence. Keep standalone technical follow-ups in engineering. Respect retired work and the current [weekly worklist/publication state](../../../../docs/worklist/oss-radar-weekly/README.md).
