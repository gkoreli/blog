## Two promises

**For a reader unfamiliar with Goga:** understand why an exciting, valuable task can still disrupt an existing commitment; distinguish saving information from preserving enthusiasm; try a concrete capture-and-review sequence; identify exceptions and ways to notice failure.

**For a reader getting to know Goga:** encounter the bedtime/computer example, the fear of losing motivation, the vague representation of displaced tasks, the attraction of a dedicated recorder, the tension with earlier essays, and the original reflection's unsupported promise that excitement will return. Keep the desire to change visible without inventing evidence that change has happened.

The new draft weaves these perspectives rather than separating the essay into a textbook and a diary. The intended roughly equal balance is an editorial judgment, not a mechanically measured word ratio or evidence of reader reception.

## Claims the revision deliberately avoids

- The five-step rule is validated, guarantees improved focus, or treats ADHD.
- Guilt predictably lowers dopamine in the described situation.
- Excitement necessarily returns, returns more strongly, or must return for an idea to be worth pursuing.
- A personal account, professional title, or number of citations establishes general expertise.
- A useful output proves that following the idea immediately was the right trade.
- Keeping routines determines personal worth, or every spontaneous change is a failure.
- Every saved idea must become work; personal interests can always be postponed behind obligations.

The thirty-minute evening in the draft is explicitly hypothetical. The one-occasion observation is a proposed way to examine the practice, not an experiment already run. The possible essay note is illustrative, not a fabricated verbatim recording.

## Walk interview: missing personal substance

The author offered roughly ten minutes of dictation. These prompts seek events and judgments not already supplied; answers need not cover every prompt equally.

1. **Tell one actual detour with a visible cost.** Beyond the bedtime example, what had you planned to do, what grabbed you, and what happened instead? What did it cost you or someone else? Approximate times are fine when labeled as estimates.
2. **Give the strongest counterexample.** When was following an exciting thought immediately genuinely worth changing the plan? What would have been lost by waiting, and which part of that is something you know rather than suspect?
3. **What actually happens to ideas you save?** Describe one you returned to, or one you never did. What did you record, what reminded you, and did the desire return? Having no successful example yet is useful information too.
4. **Where does useful work become an excuse?** In a real instance, was the detour simply more interesting, or did it let you avoid something frustrating, uncertain, or uncomfortable? What helps you tell those apart? Reject the premise if it does not fit.
5. **What do you refuse to lose while becoming more deliberate?** Which part of your spontaneity or creative life is worth protecting? Where could this rule become an excuse to keep postponing what you actually care about?
6. **Which step is likely to fail in ordinary use?** Take one realistic upcoming situation through SURGE → CAPTURE → RETURN → REVISIT → CHOOSE. Where would you probably depart from the rule, and what specific, modest boundary could make the attempt workable?
7. **What would change your mind?** What observed result would make you think the practice helps, and what would reveal that it is just another attractive system? Which claim in the draft do you disagree with or feel the agent made too neat?
