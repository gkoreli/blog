## What changed in the argument

The author supplied actual attempts with notes that went unused, not merely an imagined fear of losing motivation. Immediate exploration delivers a benefit he values. It also displaces planned work or bedtime. A title-only note omits connections and branches; fuller capture can take five to fifteen minutes and itself cause loss of the original task's context. The revised article must not answer this evidence by merely recommending richer notes with greater confidence.

The five-step practice remains a proposal. Its success now requires examining both the protected original commitment and subsequent meaningful attention to the idea. More notes alone do not establish either. The revised method distinguishes information, encountering the saved record, willingness to explore, and the cost of capture. A one-minute capture ceiling, return marker, and planned review are possible experiments, not validated parameters or actions the author has already adopted.

The evening account also reveals a time-allocation issue: work, recovery, personal projects, and sleep compete before any spontaneous idea appears. The article presents scheduling alternatives without deciding that personal interests must always lose or prescribing a new routine as if the author agreed to it.

## Title decision

Previous: **Saving Ideas Without Derailing Your Plans: A Rule I Want to Practice**.

Revised H1 and title: **Distracted by New Ideas? Why Writing Them Down Isn't Enough**.

Reader-facing promise: understand why idea capture can fail to preserve motivation or protect focus, identify which failure is occurring, and assess a bounded practice rather than accept a solved-method claim. The author contribution is the unused-note history, branching exploration, work detours, late project sessions, and unresolved value conflict. A personal suffix is not necessary to allocate equal substantive weight to those contributions.

Proposed publication slug: `distracted-by-new-ideas`. The existing draft path remains stable because the article is unpublished; no route or redirect was created. Apply the slug through the validated publication mechanism when moving into posts.

Candidate query language: *getting distracted by new ideas*, *how to stay focused when you have too many ideas*, *productive procrastination*, *losing motivation for ideas*. These are intent hypotheses, not measured keyword demand.

Qualitative search checks on 2026-09-21 surfaced adjacent usage of *productive procrastination* and *shiny object syndrome*. The latter often emphasizes novelty and unfinished projects and would impose a less accurate premise on this account, where substantial exploration can occur and the cost is displacement. No keyword-volume tool, Search Console export, ranking-difficulty data, or audience experiment was obtained. Do not call the selected phrase high-volume, low-competition, or likely to rank. A better title cannot establish distribution or traffic.

[Google Search Central's title guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/title-link) recommends descriptive, concise text, discourages keyword stuffing, and explains that title links help users assess relevance. It supports the clarity criterion, not a forecast for this article. The Earned-Trust Writing skill now explicitly requires reader language, discovery route, concrete payoff, and earned scope before accepting a title.

## Evidence checked for this revision

- [Gilbert, Strategic offloading of delayed intentions into the external environment](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1080/17470218.2014.972963), 2015. Full article available. Four online experiments, 1,196 participants; external reminders improved performance on delayed-intention tasks. No claim that the tasks preserved creative enthusiasm. The distinction between remembering an intention and knowing its content is relevant but does not validate this synthesis.
- [Masicampo and Baumeister, Consider it done!](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21688924/), 2011. Primary study abstract inspected. Specific plans reduced measured unfinished-goal interference. No detailed methods, replication review, or evidence for the combined five-step routine is claimed here.
- [University of Washington's account of Leroy and Glomb's research](https://www.washington.edu/news/2018/01/16/task-interrupted-a-plan-for-returning-helps-you-move-on/), 2018. Institution-issued report with author explanation. Ready-to-resume plans improved performance on the interrupting task; performance after returning to the original task was explicitly not tested. Do not reverse that direction when discussing a return marker. Paper DOI: 10.1287/orsc.2017.1184. This revision did not inspect the paywalled paper's full text.
- [Berridge and Kringelbach, Pleasure systems in the brain](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4425246/), 2015. Open author manuscript inspected. Wanting, pleasure, and learning are distinguished. This does not measure the author's dopamine or establish a way to reproduce his excitement.

The Oura reference remains an author report of checking his sleep and noticing poorer mornings. No wearable records were retrieved or analyzed, no matched comparison was made, and no melatonin mechanism is asserted. The transcript's hormonal statement is preserved as source material rather than promoted into a factual explanation. Dates, numeric sleep metrics, medication effects, and diagnoses were not invented or added.

## Remaining question

The author has explained why following excitement feels worthwhile, why title-only notes fail him, and why longer capture itself has a cost. Do not ask those questions again as if unanswered.

One useful next question: **When you follow an idea, what makes exploration feel complete enough to stop: reaching a concrete answer, recording the branches, making something, or running out of interest? Walk through how that stopping point works for you.**

This could clarify whether a bounded stopping condition is possible without assuming a timer or richer note solves the problem. It should remain a question, not an invented motive or result.
